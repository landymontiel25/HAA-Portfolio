What I built

I built Landmark Hunters, an app that helps you find new places and learn about them. You check in at real landmarks and rate them. Mapr, the AI planner, learns your taste from those ratings and suggests places you haven't tried. You can add landmarks that are missing. I built the entire platform using Claude Code, and the decisions were all mine.

Challenges

Problem 1: Users would sign onto the app and Mapr, our AI planner, would have no idea what they like.

Solution: Right after signing up, users get a Tinder-style card carousel. Each card shows a general category, like museums, sports, or dining, broken into specifics like steak, sushi, art, history, tennis, or pickleball. Users swipe to say whether they'd go. I timed 7 people completing it, and the average was 64 seconds.

Problem 2: Firestore lets the app write data straight from a user's phone without checking who is writing it. Anyone could fake their own points, overwrite someone else's leaderboard, or friend themselves to a stranger to read their private reviews.

Solution: I rewrote the Firestore rules so points can only increase through app actions. Every field is locked to the account that owns it, and friend connections only happen from an accepted request on both sides.

Problem 3: My family, who would use the app, wouldn't know how to operate it, and one of them suggested a tutorial.

Solution: I made a tutorial in order for any new user to understand the app as they get on, which takes no longer than 3 minutes to complete.

From game to planner

Creation: After a family trip to Europe, my dad had the idea to turn travel into education, where we could learn about places before we go instead of after, so we could really experience these places. This past summer of 2026, my family went to Africa, and by the end of the trip, I realized how useful something like this would have been. I found my old notes on it, decided it was worth building, and we created Landmark Hunters, a game where you can check into landmarks and earn points.

Transition: The transition into a planner happened during my philosophy class because I was really bored, and I started thinking about what this could become if enough people used it. Every check-in and rating would be us collecting data on what someone actually likes. I realize that time saved and discovering new places is what's valuable in today's world, and based off of this, I created Mapr and the overall vision for this app now. About 10 days in, my uncle and cousin tried it and saw no value. A week later I showed my uncle and cousin again, and they were much more interested. If it didn't work for anyone else, it would work for me.

I changed my dad's vision to build it for myself because, in college, my friends and I would always go to the same restaurants, the same dorms, the same fields, and these repetitive places got very boring very fast. If there was an app that knew what I liked and could tell me what I would like to do that's new, I would use it.

Background

Landmark Hunters is my second project, and I've never put more time into anything than this, because of how useful it can be for me daily. So far, it's been used by me and my family while I finish building it.

I fix causes, not symptoms. When duplicate landmarks let people farm points, I could have deleted them by hand. I built detection into Add a Landmark so duplicates get caught before they exist. When a feature stopped being useful, I rebuilt it in an hour. New users need about a week to build a taste profile, and most leave before then, so I built a simulator with synthetic users and ran 150 simulations to improve Mapr before real users showed up.

Proudest features and what's next

My 3 proudest features

  1. Taste score engine. Every time a user rates a place, the app adds or takes away points from the category. Mapr reads that score to decide what to suggest the user. Old ratings matter less over time, so the user's tastes can change, and the app keeps up. It also factors in distance and time of day, so it won't send you somewhere far when something close fits just as well, and it leans toward food picks around lunch and dinner.
  2. Live turn by turn navigation. While users are walking, the app tracks real GPS position and figures out where the user is on the route, then tells them the next turn.
  3. On device habit detection. The app watches where a user goes over time. If they show up in the same small area on three separate days, it decides that's a habit spot, like a restaurant or coffee shop they always go to, and uses that to make better suggestions.

What's next

  • We launch. I find out what users want, what they like, and what they don't. Then I adapt. I build what they ask for, fix what they dislike, and leave alone what works.

How to get the first 1,000 users (idea not confirmed yet)

MaprMiami. MaprMiami is a free, 7-day citywide contest I'm planning to run in Miami to launch the app. Players check in at real landmarks around the city and rate them, competing for cash prizes based on how many different places they visit and how honestly they rate them, not just raw check-ins. Inviting a friend or forming a team earns bonus points, so it spreads through word of mouth instead of ads.

On day 7, every player has to let Mapr pick 10 places for them based on how they rated all week, then rate those picks too. That's not optional if they want to compete for the final prize, so every contest player ends up actually using the planner at least once, not just checking into landmarks. Once they see Mapr get their taste right, that's the hook that keeps them using it after the contest ends.

WHY I BUILT IT

I saw on Fizz (college social media platform) that people kept asking for a dating website for Villanova students only. The day I planned to build one, someone launched one 30 minutes before me. I looked at it and thought the design was stiff and hard to click through. I thought I could build a smoother one.

WHAT RESEARCH TAUGHT ME

People want apps that work the way every other app works. I used Tinder-style swiping because my generation already knows it. I used the same bottom bar as TikTok and Instagram: (from left to right) feed, search, messages, profile. Nobody has to learn anything.

WHAT I DECIDED MYSELF

  • Crush List: Most students don't join a dating app to meet strangers. They are looking for one specific person. So I let you put that person on a Crush List. If they put you on theirs, you match automatically. You can list up to 10 people and change the list 3 times a week. The list is encrypted, so I cannot read who picked whom.
  • Anonymous likes: you can like someone without them knowing who liked them. They see you in their next 5 cards. So users won't know who matches them unless they match with each other.

HOW I BUILT IT

I built VDate with Claude Code. I wrote the specs and made the product decisions. My first commit was on Oct 15. The app was live about 21 hours later, with 126 commits in all.

WHAT I DON'T KNOW YET

VDate does not track Day 1 or Day 7 return. It has 2 matches and 0 conversations with a reply, so I do not know whether the matching loop works. Many people start sign-up and stop in onboarding. Fixing onboarding is my next job.
