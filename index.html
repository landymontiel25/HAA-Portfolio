What I built

I built the entire platform using Claude Code, and the decisions were all mine. For example, I found a bug where streaks reset at the wrong time for anyone outside UTC, sometimes hours early. I traced this to day boundaries being calculated in UTC instead of local time, and I switched the streak logic to local calendar dates.

Challenges

Problem 1: Users would sign onto the app and Mapr, our AI planner, would have no idea what they like.

Solution: Right after signing up, users get a Tinder-style card carousel. Each card shows a general category, like museums, sports, or dining, broken into specifics like steak, sushi, art, history, tennis, or pickleball. Users swipe to say whether they'd go. I timed 7 people completing it, and the average was 64 seconds.

Problem 2: Firestore lets the app write data straight from a user's phone without checking who is writing it. Anyone could fake their own points, overwrite someone else's leaderboard, or friend themselves to a stranger to read their private reviews.

Solution: I rewrote the Firestore rules so points can only increase through app actions. Every field is locked to the account that owns it, and friend connections only happen from an accepted request on both sides.

From game to planner

In 2016, after a family trip to Europe, my dad had the idea to turn travel into education, where we could learn about places before we go instead of after, so we could really experience these places. This past summer of 2026, my family went to Africa, and by the end of the trip, I realized how useful something like this would have been. I found my old notes on it, decided it was worth building, and we created Landmark Hunters, a game where you can check into landmarks and earn points.

The transition into a planner happened during class because I was really bored, and I started thinking about what this could become if enough people used it. Every check-in and rating would be us collecting data on what someone actually likes. I realize that time saved and discovering new places is what's valuable in today's world, and based off of this, I created Mapr and the overall vision for this app now.

I changed his vision to build it for myself because, in college, my friends and I would always go to the same restaurants, the same bars, the same dorms, and these repetitive places got very boring very fast. If there was an app that knew what I liked and could tell me what I would like to do that's new, I would use it.

We are not Foursquare

Foursquare logs where you've been. Landmark Hunters uses your check-ins and ratings to predict where you'll go next.

How to get the first 1,000 users

MaprMiami is a free, 7-day citywide contest I'm running in Miami to launch the app. Players check in at real landmarks around the city and rate them, competing for cash prizes based on how many different places they visit and how honestly they rate them, not just raw check-ins. Inviting a friend or forming a team earns bonus points, so it spreads through word of mouth instead of ads.

On day 7, every player has to let Mapr pick 10 places for them based on how they rated all week, then rate those picks too. That's not optional if they want to compete for the final prize, so every contest player ends up actually using the planner at least once, not just checking into landmarks. Once they see Mapr get their taste right, that's the hook that keeps them using it after the contest ends.

Proudest features and what's next

My 3 proudest features

  1. Taste score engine. Every time a user rates a place, the app adds or takes away points from the category. Mapr reads that score to decide what to suggest the user. Old ratings matter less over time, so the user's tastes can change, and the app keeps up. It also factors in distance and time of day, so it won't send you somewhere far when something close fits just as well, and it leans toward food picks around lunch and dinner.
  2. Live turn by turn navigation. While users are walking, the app tracks real GPS position and figures out where the user is on the route, then tells them the next turn.
  3. On device habit detection. The app watches where a user goes over time. If they show up in the same small area on three separate days, it decides that's a habit spot, like a restaurant or coffee shop they always go to, and uses that to make better suggestions.

What's next

  • Transition the onboarding test into the actual sign-up, and keep the test tab admin-only.
  • Add moderation for new landmarks. Anything submitted right now goes live instantly with no review, so either an AI checks it or we personally check every landmark.
  • Build a background job for dual streaks. Right now, a streak only closes when someone opens the app. If one person is offline all day, it can break for no reason.
